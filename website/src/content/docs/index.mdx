## Why LiteInjector

Evaluating countermeasures against fault injection attacks usually means long RTL simulation campaigns, or physical attack benches. LiteInjector instruments your design instead: the faults are emulated in the FPGA, on the real SoC running real software, and every attack parameter can be changed at runtime from the host.

<CardGrid stagger>
	<Card title="Any signal of your SoC" icon="puzzle">
		Probe any Migen signal, or any instrumented signal of a Verilog IP, with a few lines added to a LiteX SoC.

	<Card title="Precise triggers" icon="seti:clock">

## How it works

<Steps>

1. **Add LiteInjector to your SoC** and choose the signals to probe: the instruction bus, a security flag, a protection signal...

    ```python
    self.submodules.injector = LiteInjector([ibus.adr, ibus.dat_r], depth=16, csr_csv="injector.csv")
    self.add_uartbone("liteinjector")
    ```

2. **Describe the attack from the host**: when to inject, and which fault to apply.

    ```console
    $ liteinjector_cli run --when ibus_adr=0x0000037A --offset 1 \
          --fault bit-value:ibus_dat_r=0x00050C63 --wait 5
    ```

3. **Observe the result**, or let a campaign sweep the parameters and classify every run.

    ```console
    $ liteinjector_cli campaign campaign.toml
    Campaign 'bios-unlock-bitflip': 32 run(s).
    [1/32] {'bit': 0} -> <outcome>
    ...
    Results written to bios_unlock_results.csv
    ```

</Steps>
